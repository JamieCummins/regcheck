Method. Participants. Sample size was determined before any data collection: a power analysis in G*Power 3.1 (independent-samples t-test, d = 0.50, alpha = .05 two-tailed, power = .90) indicated that 172 participants were required. We recruited exactly 172 adult native English speakers via Prolific and randomly assigned them by computer to the retrieval-practice condition (n = 86) or the restudy condition (n = 86). No participant failed more than one of the three embedded attention checks, so no data were excluded.

Procedure and measures. In a single online session, participants studied 40 Swahili-English word pairs. The retrieval-practice group then completed three cued-recall practice cycles with corrective feedback, whereas the restudy group re-viewed each pair for an equivalent duration. Seven days later, all participants completed a 40-item cued-recall test in which the Swahili word was presented and the English translation had to be typed. The primary outcome, specified in the study's registration before data collection, was the proportion of correctly recalled English translations on this delayed test.

Results. We tested the prediction, stated in advance in the registration, that retrieval practice would yield higher delayed recall than restudy (H1). Cued-recall accuracy was higher in the retrieval-practice condition (M = .54, SD = .18) than in the restudy condition (M = .44, SD = .19), Welch's t(169.3) = 3.54, p < .001, d = 0.54, 95% CI [0.24, 0.85]. The result supports the preregistered hypothesis.
