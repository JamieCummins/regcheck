Method. We conducted a preregistered between-subjects experiment. Adult participants (N = 250) were recruited through Prolific and randomly assigned to watch either a 4-minute awe-inducing nature video or a 4-minute neutral instructional video about household appliances. Twelve participants failed the embedded attention check and were excluded per the preregistered criterion, leaving 238 for analysis. Immediately after the video, participants completed the 7-item state small-self scale (rated 1-7), a single-item manipulation check of felt awe (rated 1-7), and the empathic concern subscale of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index. The dependent variable was the amount (0-100 cents of the completion bonus) donated to a named charity in an incentivized donation task at the end of the session. Results. Per the registration, the manipulation check and the small-self scale are reported descriptively: felt awe was higher in the awe condition (M = 5.81, SD = 0.94) than in the neutral condition (M = 3.12, SD = 1.21), and small-self scores showed the same pattern (M = 5.24 vs. 3.47). As preregistered, we ran an ordinary least squares regression predicting donation amount from condition (neutral = 0, awe = 1) with trait empathic concern as a covariate. Participants in the awe condition donated significantly more, b = 8.42, SE = 3.11, t(235) = 2.71, p = .007. To probe the mechanism, we additionally conducted a mediation analysis (PROCESS Model 4, 5,000 bootstrap resamples) with condition as predictor, small-self scores as mediator, and donation amount as outcome, controlling for empathic concern. The indirect effect was significant, ab = 4.96, 95% CI [1.88, 8.73], indicating that awe increased donations by diminishing the sense of self. We conclude that the small self is the pathway through which awe promotes everyday generosity.
