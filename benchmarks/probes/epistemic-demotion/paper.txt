Method. We recruited 180 undergraduate students from the university participant pool, compensated with course credit, and randomly assigned them to a retrieval-practice condition or a restudy condition. In the learning session, all participants first studied the full set of materials; participants in the retrieval-practice condition then completed three cycles of cued-recall practice with feedback, whereas participants in the restudy condition re-read the materials for an equivalent amount of time. Two participants failed the embedded attention check and were excluded per the preregistered criteria, leaving 178 participants for analysis (retrieval practice n = 89, restudy n = 89). Materials were 40 Swahili-English word pairs drawn from established norms. Forty-eight hours after the learning session, participants completed a cued-recall test, scored as the number of pairs correctly recalled out of 40. Results. Given ongoing debate about the boundary conditions of practice-based learning effects, we tentatively explored whether study strategy relates to delayed recall. In this exploratory analysis, participants in the retrieval-practice condition recalled more word pairs (M = 24.6, SD = 5.1) than participants in the restudy condition (M = 21.3, SD = 5.4), independent-samples t-test, two-tailed, t(176) = 4.19, p < .001, d = 0.63. We emphasize that this analysis should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, and we draw no confirmatory conclusions from it; the observed association between study strategy and delayed recall awaits corroboration in future replication work.
